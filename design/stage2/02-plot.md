# Stage 2 — Plot & Narrative (1946 → 1971, Director of a computing lab)

Owner: plot / narrative designer. Proposal for the lead. The historian owns dates and facts (every dated
line is a draft to check); systems owns all numbers; card names are placeholders.

## 1. The emotional arc

**Stage 1 was urgency, weight, war.** Speed meant "lives saved"; its key line was *"We were not fast enough."*
**Stage 2 is wonder and craft, under a secret.** The war is over and nobody is dying if you are slow.
So what is speed for now? **Speed means getting there first: ahead of the world, not just ahead of the enemy.**
A forecast is worth something only if it arrives before the rain. An election result only if it comes before
the votes are counted. A landing only if the course correction is computed before the fuel runs out.
In Stage 1 being slow cost a convoy. In Stage 2 being fast makes things possible that were simply impossible.

Shape of the stage (five moods, in order):
1. **Silence (1946).** You did this before anyone, and you may never say so. The Americans get the
   headlines. A quiet, private pride. The player knows; the world does not.
2. **Craft (1946–52).** Hands-on, tinkering, valves that glow and burn out. The machine remembers its own
   program; the counter's word changes. Joy of making.
3. **Foresight (1950–52).** The machine gets ahead of the weather, then ahead of people. First wonder, first
   unease: *the machine knew before we did.*
4. **Shadow (1952–54).** Turing. The secret that protected the country now hides what it owes one man.
   The one heavy beat of the stage, and it is quiet.
5. **Scale and lift (1957–69).** Transistors, mainframes, memos, budgets, a space race. You stop touching
   machines and start signing for them. It all funnels into one goal you could not have imagined in 1946:
   a computer that flies to the Moon and lands on time.

**The rhyme with Stage 1 (the spine of this stage):** the late decrypt in 1942 ("four hours too late")
is answered by the Moon landing in 1969: the computer is overloaded, drops the small jobs, keeps the
important ones, and is *on time*. Stage 1 ends in grief that we were slow; Stage 2 ends in relief that
we were fast enough. The player should feel the 1942 line close, without the log saying so explicitly.

## 2. The opening minute

State at hand-over: the Stage 1 machines have faded, the Legacy line (about 20,000 /s) is running,
the counter still holds what was left after "Win the war", and the last log line is
*"Philadelphia, February 1946 … You think: we did that last year."*

**What is on screen at 0:00** (numbers are placeholders for systems) (after "Pass the ledger on" turns the page):
```
 1946 · Director of a computing lab
            1.2 million checks             ← unchanged label, still "checks"
            Legacy: 20,000 checks per second
        [ Run a calculation ]
 Goal: Compute N checks in total to put a computer on the Moon
 ───────────────────────────────────────────
 February 1946. A university gives you two empty rooms, a budget, and no machine.
 You cannot tell them you have done this before.
```
- **The first click: "Run a calculation".** It must feel big, not like Stage 1's +1. Each press adds a
  slice of your speed (systems: ~1 s of speed per click to start), so the floater reads **+20,000**.
  The first press is a jolt: your hand is now worth what a whole hut was worth a year ago.
- **0:03, log reacts to the first click:** *"First job: a firing table for the army. Your old clerks would
  have needed a month. It took one press."* (scale made personal, and a small shadow: still military work).
- **0:15:** "Build a computer" appears; affordable by ~0:25 (systems: ~20 s of Legacy). The player buys a
  machine of their own for the first time as a builder, not a codebreaker.
- **0:30, log:** *"Eighteen thousand valves, glowing. When one dies, you find it by the dark."* (lamp motif)
- **0:45:** the first upgrade card is visible ("Hire programmers", see §4 for the clerk who answers the advert).
- **0:60:** clicked, built, eyeing a card with its own "~15 s". Hands-on and new despite 20,000 /s running.

The goal bar shows one unit from second 0. The Moon in 1946 is meant to look absurd; a 0:50 log line winks at it.

### 3a. The renaming beat (2:50, June 1948)
On buying "Keep the program in memory":
- *"21 June 1948, Manchester. A small machine runs a program it keeps in its own memory. Its builders don't
  count checks. They count operations."*
- 3 s later, the counter's word crossfades **checks → operations**. The number does not move. The goal bar,
  Legacy line and card costs change word in the same frame (no drain or refill: it is the same goal).
- 3 s later: *"Same number. New word. You have been counting operations since 1939."*
Feeling: not a new resource, a new understanding: the player sees their whole war in the number.

### 3b. The "wait, what?" moment (1952 election)
Two lines, 8 s apart:
- *"Election night, 1952. With a few percent of the votes in, a computer predicts a landslide. The TV network
  thinks it is broken, and tells the nation the race is close."*
- *"It was a landslide. The machine was right. It was only early."*
Why it works: the player rereads it. Speed is no longer about catching up with events: the machine can
*arrive first*, and people don't trust it. It quietly plants the unease of the late game (Stage 7).

### 3c. Turing's fate (1954), handled with weight
The player met him in Stage 1 (*"A shy mathematician from Cambridge arrives by train"*) and saw his
scoring sheets. That set-up is what makes this land.
- The engine holds every other log line and reveal for ~20 s before and ~25 s after (no new card, no sound;
  production continues, since speed never drops). The only thing that happens is the line.
- The line: *"7 June 1954. Alan Turing is found dead at his home. He was forty-one. You knew what he did in
  the war. So did the people who prosecuted him. You signed the same paper he did, and you say nothing."*
- Then nothing. No upgrade is tied to it, no number changes, no card mentions him. Do not dramatise the method
  of death (the inquest's verdict is disputed; historian to confirm wording).
- Seeds for later stages: 2009 government apology, 2013 pardon.

### 3d. Finale 1, "Land on the Moon" (the rhyme with 1942)
On click, three lines, 3 s apart:
1. *"20 July 1969. Three minutes from the surface, the Moon computer flashes an alarm: 1202. It is overloaded."*
2. *"It drops the small jobs and keeps the ones that matter. The crew are told: go."*
3. *"Twenty-five seconds of fuel left. Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed. It was fast enough."*
Never mention 1942. Players who remember *"We were not fast enough"* will hear it.

### 3e. Ending and handover into Stage 3
`ending` (played in order, a few seconds apart):
- *"1970. The Moon budget is cut. The mainframes are leased to an airline and a bank."*
- *"Your first clerk retires. She leaves you her pencil."* (motif closes, §4)
- *"The machines are going out of date. What you learned is not."* (same shape as Stage 1's line)

`next` (after "Pass the ledger on", Stage 3 opens on it):
*"California, November 1971. A company sells a whole computer's brain on one chip the size of a fingernail.
It costs less than a month's rent. In a garage, someone is already heating a soldering iron."*

## 4. Recurring motifs (4 touches, light)

1. **The clerk who misses her pencil.** 0:50 (1946, "Hire programmers"): *"Your first clerk from 1939 answers
   the advert. She is a programmer now. She keeps a pencil behind her ear anyway."* 10:35 (1962):
   *"Before his flight, the astronaut asks for the woman with the pencil to check the computer's numbers. If
   she says they're good, he'll go."* 14:00: she retires and leaves you the pencil. (Stage 3 can open with it on the desk.)
2. **The weather.** 1950: the forecast takes as long as the weather. The speedometer is the forecast (§6), so
   the player watches it shrink below one day: *the forecast now arrives before the rain*. One log line when it
   first drops under 1 hour: *"The forecast is ready before the forecasters have had lunch."*
3. **The lamp.** Valves glowing; finding the dead valve by the dark (0:30); Earthrise as one lamp in the dark (goal).
4. **The tea and the mug.** The tea-company computer (1951) quietly answers the chained mug of 1939. One line, no more.

## 5. The voice of the log

**1946 → 1958: the lab notebook.** First person plural, measurements, small dry jokes, no exclamation marks.
**1959 → 1971: the memo.** Headers, initials, departments, "per your request". Warmth leaks out between the lines.
The switch happens on the transistor card (9:20), so the player feels the lab become an organisation.

Sample lines:
1. *"Valve 4,112 out again. Replaced. Machine up 61 hours this week, a record. Morale: good; coffee: worse."*
2. *"Ran the same sum twice to be sure. Got two answers. Ran it a third time. We now have opinions."*
3. *"The new memory is a tube of mercury. It holds a thousand numbers and has to be kept at exactly 40 °C. Like a baby."*
4. *"MEMO. To: Director. From: Payroll. Re: the computer. It has paid 9,000 staff without error. Please stop it from doing it on Fridays at 4:55."*
5. *"MEMO. Re: your request for a second mainframe. Approved. Please stop calling it 'she'. — R.T., Finance"*
6. *"MEMO. To: all staff. The Moon computer may not be visited. It is being woven. Shoes off in the clean room."*

## 6. Notes for the other designers

**Systems (pacing feel)**
- **The click must matter at 0:00.** With 20,000 /s already running, a +1 click is an insult. Click = a
  share of speed (start ~1 s worth, falling to ~0.1 s as producers grow; holding the button must not dominate).
- **First purchase by ~0:25**, first upgrade by ~0:50. The opening minute is the place to be generous.
- **Legacy should be overtaken around 3:30–4:00** so the log line *"outrun everything you did in the war"* is true.
- **Leave a gap at Turing:** no reveal 20 s before and 25 s after (counts as story, not dead time).
- **The choice (5:30)**, story framing "Payroll for a tea company": *Sell to business* (mainframes/computers get
  cheaper) vs *Train programmers* (each click adds more of your speed). Business vs people, like Stage 1.
- **Late upgrades** ("Chips", "Weave the memory by hand") keep the ≤45 s rule in the last stretch before the goal.
- **No hiring after the goal** ("the mission is locked"). Finale costs: Land, then Bring them home.
- **Goal naming:** the click lands at Apollo 8 (orbit), so "Put a computer on the Moon" is fulfilled by Finale 1. If
  that feels like cheating, rename the goal card "Build the Moon computer" and keep the bar sentence as is.

**UX (the era change)**
- **Page turn, not a new screen.** Same layout; skin goes from 1939 paper to a 1946 lab (cream graph paper,
  green-grey ink, notebook log font). At the 1959 memo switch the log becomes typewriter memos. Panels never move.
- **Keep the word "checks" for the first ~3 minutes** so the rename lands. The rename is a 600 ms crossfade of the
  word only, everywhere at once (counter, goal bar, Legacy, costs, speed). The number must not flicker or re-animate.
- **Legacy line:** grey, plain, under the speed. Fades to lower contrast once new machines overtake it; never removed.
- **Speedometer task:** "Forecasting tomorrow's weather: about 1 day" at the start, shrinking to minutes by the
  Moon. Plain, true to the 1950 beat, and it says what speed means in this stage: being early.
- **Finale:** the 3 Moon lines should get the full log width and no other events; respect reduced motion.
