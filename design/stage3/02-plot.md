# Stage 3 — Plot & Narrative (Nov 1971 → Jan 1984, the garage)

Owner: plot / narrative designer. Proposal for the lead. The historian owns dates and facts (lines marked
*(check)* need a look); systems owns every number and timing; card names are placeholders.

## 1. The emotional arc

- **Stage 1 = urgency.** Speed is lives. *"We were not fast enough."*
- **Stage 2 = wonder.** Speed is getting there first. *"This time, we were fast enough."*
- **Stage 3 = joy, then giving it away.** Speed stops being scarce. It leaves the guarded building and goes
  into kitchens, bedrooms and classrooms. The question is no longer "fast enough?" but "fast for whom?".
  The answer this stage gives: for everyone. One cold shadow near the end plants the next question:
  **fast is not the same as right** (Petrov, §5). That seeds the unease of the later AI stages.

Five moods, in order:
1. **Small again (1971–74).** From three floors to one bench. A chip under a fingernail. Tinkering joy.
2. **The club (1975–77).** Strangers in a garage share everything: schematics, programs, tape.
3. **Money (1976–80).** The hobby becomes a business. Sharing or selling? A company, then shares.
4. **Everywhere (1981–83).** Computers in homes, offices, schools. A magazine's Person of the Year is a machine.
5. **Shadow, then "Hello" (1983–84).** A machine is fast and wrong; a human says no. Then a computer
   speaks on stage, and the whole hall stands up.

**The spine (the rhyme for this stage):** the first clerk checked one guess at a time with a pencil, in a
hut nobody could talk about. Her grandchild ends the stage putting more computing into ordinary houses
than the whole of Bletchley Park ever had, and the secret is finally out. The pencil goes home.

## 2. Who you are, and why a garage

**You are the first clerk's grandchild.** Stage 2 ends with her retiring and leaving the director her pencil.
"Pass the ledger on" now really passes it: the old director, looking at the 4004 advert and thinking of
three floors, gives the logbook, the pencil and the advert to someone young enough to use them.
(The 1939 codebreaker would be about 54 in 1971: possible, but garages belong to the young.
It also gives the game its first real handover between people, which the ledger was always about.)

- **Why a garage:** you are nineteen, a chip costs less than a dinner, and your parents' garage is the
  only room you have. The big companies don't want small computers; nobody has asked for one yet.
- **Money from Stage 2:** story preference: it stays with the division. "You take the logbook, not the
  budget." You keep the Legacy row: "The computing lab, 1946–71 · N per second, forever." (Systems decides.)
- **Promotions (header role):** "Hobbyist in a garage" → **"Founder"** (first order of fifty boards, 1976)
  → **"Chief executive"** (the company goes public, 1980). Stage 4 starts as "Software company CEO", so the
  role continues without a jump.

## 5. Quiet moments, and how to hold them

**The Petrov beat (the one heavy line).** Not a death, but the closest the stage comes to one, and it
rhymes with two earlier lines: 1942 (we were slow and people died) and election night 1952 (the machine
was right and the men doubted it). Here the machine is fast, sure and wrong, and a man doubts it.
> *"September 1983. A Soviet computer reports five American missiles in the air. The officer on duty,
> Stanislav Petrov, decides it is wrong. It is. Nobody hears about it for fifteen years."*

Handle like Turing's death: `hush: 20` (no cards, no other lines), 12 s after a purchase, never attached
to a purchase or a reward, no exclamation, no comment from "you". The ledger does not explain the rhyme.

**The grandmother (beats 4 and 16).** Warm, not heavy. Beat 4 releases Stage 2's secret ("you still cannot
say it"): the family finally hears it at breakfast. Beat 16 is the one sad fact of the decade: the home
computer was sold to boys, and the women who did this work first were written out (CS degrees for women
peak around 1984, then fall *(check)*). One line, her voice, no lecture. No hush needed; give it 10 s alone.
She does not die in this stage. If she ever does, it belongs to a later keeper's ledger.

## 6. Goal moment and finale

- **Goal (total-production milestone, clicked when ready): "Put a computer in every home"** (systems may
  pick the exact name; "Sell a computer to everyone" reads as a slogan). Ledger, Jan 1983:
  *"January 1983. A magazine chooses its Person of the Year. This year it is not a person. It is the computer."*
- After the goal the team is complete: *"The factory is full. Every operation now goes to the last two jobs."*
- **Finale 1: "Put a computer in every classroom"** (1983). Ledger: *"Schools unpack computers by the
  thousand. A class of ten-year-olds writes its first program before lunch: 10 PRINT "HELLO". 20 GOTO 10."*
- **Finale 2: "Make a computer anyone can use"** (ends the stage, Jan 1984). Ledger: *"24 January 1984.
  On a stage, a computer with a mouse and pictures instead of commands is lifted out of a bag. It says, in its own voice: Hello."*

## 8. The new mechanic that makes the best story: **computers that leave home**

Recommendation (one): **you sell the computers themselves, and they keep counting after they are sold.**
Stage 2 sold *time* on machines you kept. Stage 3 sells the *machine*: it leaves your garage, goes into
someone's house and keeps adding to your total there, forever ("Computers in homes: N"). The new verb is
letting go; the decision is the **price**: cheap puts more machines into homes (the goal), dear brings money
for the next factory. That is the whole story of the decade in one control: the club's ideal against the
company's books. The home counter is the goal's natural unit, so the goal bar reads like the story.

Its ledger lines fire at home-count thresholds (not on a timer), so each player's sales write the story:
- 1 home: *"Your first buyer is a dentist in Ohio. He does his bills on it, and after dark he plays a game about landing on the Moon."*
- 100: *"A farmer keeps his herd on one. He writes to say the cows have not noticed."*
- 1,000: *"A school buys one. The waiting list to use it is longer than the one for the football team."*
- 10,000: *"A girl of twelve writes a game on hers. The shop sells it in plastic bags by the till."*
- 100,000: *"A writer finishes a novel on one. She says she will never go back to the typewriter."*
- 1,000,000: *"A million of your computers are switched on in other people's houses. You will never meet
  most of their owners. They are all still counting."*
- On cutting the price (once): *"You cut the price. The letters from parents double. So does the accountant's frown."*

The last line echoes Stage 1's handover ("the papers burned. The count is not."): the count has escaped every building.
