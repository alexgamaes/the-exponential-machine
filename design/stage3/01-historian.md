# Stage 3 — The Garage (Nov 1971 → Jan 1984): historian

Historian's proposal for Stage 3. Facts from memory; items marked (check) need a look before they ship.

## 1. Who you are
- **The same keeper.** The Bletchley codebreaker is about 55 in 1971, runs three floors of machines, and reads the 4004
  advert. Recommendation: **they quit.** A pensioner in a garage among twenty-year-olds is funnier and truer than
  another young founder, and it keeps the ledger's one voice. The garage belongs to the neighbour's teenager, who
  appears in the ledger now and then and takes the ledger at the end (section 7).
- **Money resets** (Stage 2's dollars belonged to the division). Opening line: "You leave the division the building,
  the mainframes and the cafeteria. You take the pencil." First cash comes from selling something, as it really did.
- **Roles (promotions):** *Garage founder* → *Founder and president* (when home computers open, 1977: the company
  gets a name and an office) → *Chairman of the board* (the IPO beat, Dec 1980). Plain, era-true, no brand names.

## 2. Producers, goal, finale, choice, speedometer
- **Producer 1: Kits** — "Ship a kit". A board, a bag of chips, a weekend with a soldering iron. From the start.
- **Producer 2: Home computers** — "Ship a computer". In a box, plug it into the television. Unlocked by the 1977 card.
  Both make operations because they are out there, running, in other people's homes. (A third, "Office computers",
  after the IBM PC card, only if systems need it. I would not add it: the PC works better as a card or a customer.)
- **Goal: "A computer in every home"** (plain name; Gates's and Microsoft's line was "on every desk and in every home").
  Total-production milestone, clicked when ready. Reward ledger line: Time's Machine of the Year (issue dated Jan 1983).
- **Finale (2 paid actions, Jan 1984):** *Air the advert* (22 Jan 1984, a hammer through a screen, shown once during
  the Super Bowl) → *Unveil the new computer* (24 Jan 1984: it takes itself out of a bag and says hello). Ends the stage.
- **The choice (1977): "Open or closed".** Real: Wozniak insisted the Apple II have eight expansion slots; Jobs wanted two.
  Open won in 1977 and in 1981 (the IBM PC was built from other people's parts, and was cloned by 1982–83); closed
  came back with the Macintosh in 1984. Both paths are real and both made fortunes.
  - **Open: eight slots** — other people build add-ons and programs: demand grows with every computer already out there.
  - **Closed: one sealed box** — you keep the whole price: each computer earns 1.5× more money (or costs less to make).
  - Backups: *Kits or assembled* (1976: the Byte Shop orders fifty Apple Is "fully assembled, please"; Sinclair sold the
    ZX80 both ways, £79.95 or £99.95) · *Hobbyists or business* (1979, VisiCalc).
- **Speedometer: "Drawing one frame of a computer-made film."** 1982 is the year of Tron and Star Trek II's Genesis
  sequence; frames took hours on the best machines (check exact figures; size set by systems, ~5 h → ~15 s).
  Comparison: **"as fast as N Cray-1s"** (1976, ~1.6×10⁸ peak). The legacy starts at a fraction of one Cray-1, so the
  first "You are now faster than a Cray-1" is a natural milestone beat. Backup task: "Thinking ten moves ahead in chess"
  (Ken Thompson's Belle, 1980–83).

## 3. The new mechanic (candidate): **you set the price**
A dial on the computer you sell: e.g. **$1,295 · $595 · $299 · $99** (Apple II 1977 · C64 1982 · Atari/TI 1983 · ZX81
in dollars 1981). Customers buy home computers **by themselves** at a rate set by your price and by demand; every
sale pays money and adds a machine to the homes count, and machines in homes make operations.
- **The trade-off:** cheap = more homes, more operations, but little money for the next card; dear = money for chips,
  disks and factories, but fewer homes. The best setting changes as demand arrives (customers, as in Stage 2).
- **Why it fits 1971–84:** this era *is* the price collapse. The 8080 cost $360 in 1974; the 6502 was $25 in 1975.
  The Apple II was $1,298; the C64 was $595 in toy shops; TI cut the 99/4A to under $100 and left the business in 1983.
  Tramiel: "Computers for the masses, not the classes." The founder's real daily decision was the price tag.
- **Why it differs from Stage 2:** Stage 2 split *your output* between two balances. Here you never press "buy" on the
  second producer: the market does, and the verb is *pricing*. A new kind of decision, still easy for a bot (try each
  price, keep the best).
- **Demand events** reuse Stage 2's customers: hobbyists, the first computer shop, schools, accountants (VisiCalc),
  families, Britain, offices (IBM PC). Software is what raises demand: people bought Apple IIs to run VisiCalc.
- Fallback mechanic if pricing proves too close to Stage 2: **type-in listings** (a magazine prints your program;
  readers type it in; the user base grows by itself between your purchases).

## 5. Ending lines (after the finale)
- 1984-01: "The box says hello. People who have never touched a computer want one."
- 1984-02: "Offices fill with the other kind: beige, open, cloned. Every one needs programs."
- 1984-03: "The neighbour's teenager, whose garage it was, writes the programs now. She asks for the ledger."

## 6. Handover to Stage 4 (1984, software company CEO)
> 1984. The machines are everywhere now, and every one of them is waiting to be told what to do.
> You hand over the ledger and the pencil. Whoever writes the instructions will run the next ten years.

(If the team keeps one keeper for all stages, swap the last sentence for: "You sell the factory. Instructions, it turns
out, cost nothing to copy.")
